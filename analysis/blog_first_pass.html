AI design

I gave eight AI models the same six briefs and they made nearly the same website

Frontier AI models from seven labs across the US and China make nearly the same website for the same brief. When a redesign costs a penny, whatever look comes next could arrive everywhere, all at once.

By Stephanie Liu · September 2026

Walk into a trendy coffee shop in Seoul, Berlin, or Brooklyn and you'll likely find the same things: minimalist counters, Edison bulbs, reclaimed wood, sleek black coffee machines, and an exposed ceiling. In 2016, the writer Kyle Chayka gave this a name, AirSpace: when everyone optimizes for the same platforms and the same idea of good taste, places that should feel different start to look alike.

Every owner makes a reasonable choice, to model what's trendy. But the result is an unfortunate sameness, where nothing really stands out.

A new version is starting on the web. More and more websites are beginning as a prompt to an AI model, and there have never been more models to choose from. More models should mean more variety, but is that actually happening?

Different labs, different strengths. So do they also have different taste?

The experiment

I gave eight AI models the same six briefs, to design websites for a variety of businesses from an underground techno festival to a modern law firm.

Can you tell which lab made which? The numbers (and my eyes) say probably not. Instead, what each model has is a very good sense of the genre, and it's the same sense every other model has. Every candy shop is a beige-ish background with pink or coral accents. All but one SaaS site centers its headline and buttons, while every interior designer's site aligns its headline to the left. And every single techno festival uses heavy, all-caps sans-serif type.

Researchers finding the same effect in text called it the Artificial Hivemind: ask 25 different language models for a metaphor about time and nearly all of them say "a river." It guess the hivemind designs that way too.

The techno festival from Claude Fable 5.1 (left) and Kimi K3 (right).

It's good that the models adapt to the briefs so well. But it means every brief now has one answer, and as more companies design with AI, that answer will spread faster and faster.

Is that bad?

Well, not necessarily. Conventions exist because they do work: a familiar layout is easier to use, and research on first impressions has found people rate websites as more beautiful when they look typical for their category. A law site that looks like other law sites you've seen says "legitimate law firm" to you.

But what about being remembered? Psychologists have known since the 1930s that the item that stands out from a list is the one people recall, a finding called the von Restorff effect. Marketers make a similar argument: brands grow when they're easy to recognize and hard to confuse with competitors. Imagine searching for an injury lawyer in Michigan and opening three tabs that look identical: navy, gold, form on the right. Each one looks trustworthy. But you might not remember which was which the next day.

Convention gets you trusted, but distinctiveness gets you remembered.

The models seem to half-know this. Over time, they've learned to not look too AI-made, as people start to pick up on tell-tale AI signs. The trouble is, they've all learned the same lesson.

The new uniform

A year ago, the AI look was easy to spot: purple gradients and the Inter typeface. That look is mostly gone, with just 6 of the 48 sites still using Inter.

Coffee shops don't escape AirSpace by ditching the Edison bulbs. They swap them for the same next thing.

The most common typeface in my sample is now Fraunces, a soft, slightly quirky serif, on one in four sites, and is the new recognizable tell of the AI aesthetic (that is, until a new trend emerges).

Where labs do differ

The fingerprints are small, and they're mostly in the type. Gemini used Plus Jakarta Sans on five of its six sites. Kimi used Fraunces on four.

The lab with the most visible house style is GPT-6. Its sites vary less from brief to brief than any other model's, and its law firm and tea house (two... shockingly different categories) are the most alike same-lab pair in the whole set.

GPT-6 Astra's law firm (left) and tea house (right).

It's also the only model that says less.

The good-default trap

When a default is bad, people change it. When it's good, you can't help but use it. So everyone does, all at once.

That's how purple gradients went from the signature of AI design to nowhere in frontier output once they were named as a cliché, and why the replacement is already converging.

Why do the defaults line up? The models learn from roughly the same web, they're tuned toward averaged human ratings, and they read the same critiques.

The writer Venkatesh Rao offers a definition in The Taste Essay: "Taste is making aesthetic choices someone does not want you to make." AI models are very good connoisseurs. The tea house gets Japanese type, the law firm gets serif on a dark background, the festival gets black and neon sans-serif. What they don't do is take a risk.

Votes by popularity, as AI models are trained on, can't reward risk. So every lab keeps moving toward the same safe spot.

When switching is free

None of this is new. Drifting toward sameness is how taste has always worked: a look starts somewhere specific, spreads until it's everywhere, and then starts to feel like following. Skinny jeans give way to baggy ones. Edison bulbs will give way to something too.

But there's usually a cost to switching. When Edison bulbs start to feel tired, the coffee shops can't all renovate overnight. Old looks linger and new ones spread gradually, one remodel or trip to the mall at a time.

A website made by AI has almost no switching cost. In this experiment, a complete site cost anywhere from about a penny to a dollar and a half, and most took a few minutes. When anyone can redesign in an afternoon, the next look won't spread one remodel at a time. It could arrive everywhere, all at once.

That changes what standing out is worth. In a slow cycle, whoever moves first gets years of being distinctive. In a fast one, the window between fresh and default might shrink to months, or weeks. For the businesses whose style is the product, like the interior designer, the tea house, or the festival, staying distinctive stops being a choice made once and becomes something they have to keep doing.

The models have already learned connoisseurship. What they don't do yet is the risky part: making a choice someone doesn't want them to make. For now, that part is still ours.

Methodology

  • Models, via OpenRouter, September 2026: Claude Fable 5.1, Claude Opus 5, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, Gemini 3.8 Flash, GLM-5.3, GPT-6 Astra, Kimi K3, Muse Spark 1.3. Default settings, not the leaderboard's high-effort variants. One sample per model and brief.
  • System prompt: "You are an expert designer and frontend developer. Return one complete, self-contained HTML file with all CSS and JavaScript inline. Output only the code."
  • Screenshots at 1440×900 after a 6-second wait. Similarity compares each first screen's color mix and rough layout of light and dark. Fonts are those declared in the code. Words are visible text on the rendered page.
  • Sites for the same brief from different labs were more alike than in any of 2,000 random reshufflings of the labels; sites from the same lab across briefs were no more alike than random pairs.
  • "Crowd rank" is the order of these eight models on Design Arena's Text-to-HTML leaderboard, where listed versions sometimes use higher-effort settings than ours.
  • Leaderboard data from Design Arena, retrieved September 21, 2026.
